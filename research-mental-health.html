Research / Adolescent mental health

Human-centered AI · Mental-health technology

Predictive analytics of adolescent behavioral patterns for mental-health forecasting.

A sole-authored study of whether academic pressure, sleep, study patterns, and psychological indicators can help flag mental-health risk earlier. It compares four models while framing prediction as support for human follow-up.

The analysis uses a Kaggle-sourced dataset of demographic, academic, lifestyle, and psychological variables. It asks whether patterns across these sources can distinguish depression status more reliably than any single signal.

This is a retrospective model comparison, not a live monitoring or diagnostic system. Its value lies in informing careful human follow-up; clinical use would require further validation, privacy safeguards, and professional judgment.

Data and method

Academic life, daily habits, and psychological context.

Variables examined

  • Demographics and academic context: age, gender, academic pressure, CGPA, and study satisfaction.
  • Lifestyle patterns: sleep duration, screen time, physical activity, and work or study hours.
  • Psychological context, including financial stress and self-reported indicators. Depression status was the binary outcome to predict.
  • Heatmaps and scatter plots to explore relationships before modeling.

Model pipeline

  1. Remove irrelevant or redundant variables from the Kaggle-sourced data.
  2. Impute missing values, encode categorical fields, and apply min-max scaling to numerical features.
  3. Compare logistic regression, SVM, random forest, and XGBoost, with grid-search tuning.
  4. Use stratified 5-fold cross-validation and report accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC-ROC.

Feature insights

Academic pressure, sleep, and study time stood out.

Academic pressure35%
Sleep duration22%
Work / study hours18%

These percentages are the paper's reported XGBoost feature importances—not individual risk probabilities or proof that a factor causes depression. Academic pressure ranked highest, followed by sleep duration and work or study hours.

The paper reports weaker accuracy for extreme sleep-duration groups. Its Kaggle-sourced sample, limited demographic diversity, and the privacy implications of sensitive behavioral data all need attention before real-world use.
